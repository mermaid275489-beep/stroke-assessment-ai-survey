From clinical scores to measurable movement

Clinical rating scales judge movement by eye. AI can measure it frame by frame. This project asks what lies between the two, and builds the bridge one scoring criterion at a time.

It begins with laboratory motion-capture data from people after stroke, continues with a survey that decomposes 57 assessment tools into features a computer can quantify, and points toward an app that turns ordinary video into a report clinicians can read.

The limits of scales

Subjective, expert-dependent, hard to repeat

Scores such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment or the Berg Balance Scale rely on trained raters, show ceiling effects, and are difficult to administer often or remotely.

The limits of end-to-end AI

Predicting the score inherits its flaws

Most systems learn to predict the clinical score directly. They inherit its subjectivity and reveal little about which movement features drive the result.

1

Data

Seeing the deviation in post-stroke gait

A pilot clinical gait study used optical motion capture to characterise how walking changes after stroke.

Joint angles of the hip, knee and ankle were compared with those of healthy controls across the whole gait cycle. Instead of reducing each curve to a single peak or range, statistical parametric mapping (SPM1D) tests every percentage point of the cycle, showing when in stance or swing a deviation occurs, not only whether it exists.

The pattern that emerged is the starting point for everything that follows: deviations are specific to phases, they differ between patients, and they appear on both sides of the body.

Survey

What can a camera actually score?

Rather than treating clinical scores as prediction targets, the survey asks how each scale’s scoring criteria decompose into movement features that AI can quantify.

We assembled a library of guideline-recommended and research-standard motor and mobility tools, then coded every scoring entry: which movement component it grades, what information distinguishes its grades, whether that information is visible in ordinary video, and whether a computed quantity could reproduce the grade boundary.

Missing information is not the main obstacle. Video could provide all score-defining information for 134 entries. What usually stands in the way is the grading of a visible movement: 73 fully available entries depend on a proxy property or an overall judgement, more than the 62 entries that are Partial or None combined.

Only 61 entries, from 27 tools, are both fully observable and directly computable; most are timed, counted or distance tests such as the 10-Metre Walk Test, Timed Up and Go, Box and Block Test and Nine-Hole Peg Test. The rest need validated thresholds, or information that a camera cannot capture: force, tone, reflexes and self-report.

App, in development

A report clinicians can read

The aim is not another black-box score. Like an ECG machine that prints an interpretable trace, the app should track movement from ordinary video, compute a task-specific feature profile, and explain each deviation, while stating plainly what the video cannot show.

Gait report

Concept prototype running on simulated kinematics. Not a medical device.

Direction 1

From curves to a diagnosis-aware gait report

Interpreting gait biomechanics is complex, and current reports are built from hand-written rules. Taking inspiration from automated ECG interpretation, we will test whether language models can describe gait phenotypes such as knee hyperextension, insufficient ankle motion or a “frozen” hip, beginning by benchmarking general-purpose models on kinematic curves.

Direction 2

Objective upper-limb tracking

Upper-limb tests such as the Action Research Arm Test and Wolf Motor Function Test depend on the therapist. Markerless capture, from multi-camera systems to a single camera running pose estimation, can make consensus tasks like drinking, planar reaching and pinching consistent and scalable, and can show how a task was completed, not only whether.

Strategy
Direction 3

Synthetic data: more examples, fewer faces

Stroke datasets are small, video records identifiable people, and labelling is slow. Synthetic movement data, generated with known labels, can help with scarcity and privacy, support model testing, and allow robotics to be simulated before any trial.

Where the project stands

2020–23

Clinical motion data collected

Optical motion capture and force-plate recordings of people with stroke, knee osteoarthritis and amputation.

2025

Pilot gait analysis

Curve-level SPM1D comparison identified reduced hip and knee flexion on the affected side and a delayed swing on the unaffected side.

2026

Survey: scales to computable features

A 57-tool library and 196 coded entries linking clinical criteria to video availability, computability and feature families.

Next

Prototype app

Video to pose tracking to a task-specific feature profile to an interpretable report, starting with gait and upper-limb consensus tasks.

Then

Validation and clinical utility

Analytical, task-level and clinical-construct validation across severity, assistance, devices and settings, followed by tests of whether the report improves clinical decisions.
